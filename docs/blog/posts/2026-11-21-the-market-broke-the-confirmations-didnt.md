## Lede

At 11:02 on Friday morning, with the S&P 500 already down two percent on the day and the Nasdaq-100 touching a bear market, Apollo and Goldman Sachs announced that they had sold roughly $44 billion of bonds and notes to finance Nvidia chips that SpaceX will lease for five years. The largest piece — $25.2 billion of senior secured notes due 2033 — carried a single-A-minus rating and a yield of 8.259 percent, about 2.3 percentage points more than the average investment-grade company pays. The deal was increased in size. It was oversubscribed. It was the largest financing of graphics processors ever done, and it priced on the day the S&P 500 fell 3.7 percent and Nvidia 14.

Hold those two facts side by side, because the whole week lives in the space between them. Three days earlier Nvidia had reported $115.8 billion of quarterly revenue, up 103 percent from a year ago, and guided the next quarter to $130 billion — 18 percent above what the Street had penciled in. The night after that, Anthropic priced the largest initial public offering in history at $83 a share, above a range the banks had set at $70 to $78, and raised $81.3 billion. Those were the three events the dashboard had registered in advance as the strongest scheduled tests of a capex bust, with real odds attached — one in four that Nvidia would guide below consensus, one in three that Anthropic would stumble, three in ten that the SpaceX deal would not price by year-end. None fired. Meanwhile the S&P 500 fell for a seventh straight session to 6,910, 11.5 percent below its high; Nvidia lost 23 percent on the week and 14 percent on Friday alone after Reuters reported that SEC enforcement staff are examining its accounting; the Taiwan index had its worst week since 1991.

That is the argument this column has to referee. The bears say the market has simply gotten ahead of a method that insists on scoring the lagging node — that when the chain that finances the AI buildout breaks, it breaks first in price and in the plumbing (conditional guarantees, receivables, funding gaps), and only later in the capex guidance and credit spreads the dashboard calls confirmation. The bulls say the method just did its job: the three things that would actually have confirmed a bust were tested in one week and passed, the bond market hedged the stock market for the first time in a month, investment-grade spreads sat under their line, and what fell was priced, not financed. The dashboard's computed weight on the bust came down from 19 to 11 percent at the six-week horizon. The next fork cluster — a CoreWeave loan by November 30, the Federal Reserve and Broadcom on December 9, Oracle around the same date — will say which reading was the lagging one.

## Terms used this week

> **Credit spread (OAS).** The extra yield a corporate bond pays over a Treasury of the same maturity, in basis points (hundredths of a percentage point); wider means lenders want more compensation. · **Real yield.** A Treasury yield after expected inflation is removed, read from inflation-protected bonds; the hurdle a project must clear in real terms. · **Breakeven.** The inflation rate at which a normal Treasury and an inflation-protected one pay the same — the market's inflation forecast. · **Non-event likelihood ratio.** The dashboard's rule that a forecast which was given real odds and did not happen must count as evidence against the branch it pointed to, in proportion to how likely it was under that branch. · **Equal-weight versus cap-weight.** An index where every company counts the same versus one where the biggest count most; the gap between them measures how narrow the market is. · **Credit-default swap (CDS).** Insurance against a company defaulting, quoted in basis points a year; above 1,000 is conventionally called distressed. · **Dispersion.** How far apart the returns of a group of stocks have spread; rising dispersion means the market has stopped treating them as one trade. · **Reaction ratio.** A stock's move the day after its results divided by the move its options had implied; the dashboard's test of whether a small piece of news produced an outsized response.

## Bulls

*The strongest case for a healthy market is that the week's three scheduled tests of the AI economy's funding — the chipmaker's guidance, the lab's flotation and the largest GPU financing ever — were set in advance, with real odds of failure, and all three passed; what fell was price, and the bond market caught it.*

### The dog that didn't bark, three times

The dashboard does not score a selloff. It scores events it named beforehand, and it has a rule — the computed non-event likelihood ratio — that when a thing it gave real odds to does not happen, that absence counts as evidence in proportion to how likely it was under the bear's world. Last week it had Nvidia guiding below consensus at 25 percent with the strongest tier of weight attached; Anthropic's IPO delayed, shrunk or priced below the top of its range at 35 percent; the SpaceX financing failing to price or being sweetened at 30 percent. Nvidia guided to $130 billion against an implied $110 billion. Anthropic priced at $83 against a $70–78 range and took $81.3 billion plus a $26 billion placement from its own suppliers. SpaceX's $44 billion priced at 8.259 percent for the senior class, inside the 7.75–8.75 percent range brokers had indicated on November 5, upsized and oversubscribed, with Moody's at A3. Each miss by the bears carries a computed ratio of 0.29 to 0.58 on the bust's odds, and together they outweigh the five negatives the dashboard did score. That is why the six-week bust weight fell from 19 to 11 in the week the index entered a correction: the three biggest tests of demand and funding were run, and the capital was there — at a price, but there. ## Bears

*The strongest case that the week was the leading edge of a capex bust is that every node the dashboard calls leading has now broken — the periphery lenses, the chain's weakest credits, the vendor's own balance sheet, and the governance of its central firm — and that the three "clean" tests were tests of demand when the question was always funding.*

### The chain is breaking from the inside, and the vendor just said so

Nvidia's results were the week's bear case dressed as a beat. Revenue up 103 percent, guidance up 18 percent above consensus — and receivables up 28.5 percent in a quarter against revenue up 20.4 percent, inventory up 26 percent, receivables now 70 cents of every dollar of a quarter's sales, up from 65. Its disclosed supply and capacity commitments had already jumped from $119 billion to $279 billion by July. On the call, the interim chief executive said the company's financing commitments to the smaller cloud providers that buy its chips had been cut from $36 billion to $32 billion and would now take effect only once a provider "secures the funding they need" and brings capacity online — a guarantee that, in her words, "doesn't exist anymore" if a project stalls — and that Nvidia would be "far more selective" about such deals. In the dashboard's language that is a vendor-financing commitment materially shrunk and made conditional, which fired a registered fork, and it is the second game's defining move: a supplier underwriting its customers' credit before it will invest. Two days later Reuters reported that SEC enforcement staff are looking at "potential accounting issues" at the company; the board had said a day earlier that Jensen Huang will not return. The stock fell 14 percent on Friday and 23 percent on the week. Lucent and Nortel financed their own demand in 1999 and 2000; Cisco's receivables and inventory outran its revenue for two quarters before the penny miss. The bear does not need the guide to be wrong. It needs the receivables to be the guide. ## Pigs

A pig, in Stanley Druckenmiller's sense, is a position with an asymmetric payoff — where being right pays several times what being wrong costs — held with conviction while the asymmetry lasts. The Wall Street cliché is that pigs get slaughtered; his point was that it takes courage to be one, and the difference is whether the asymmetry is real. Every item below states what it pays if right and what it costs if wrong, and is graded next week against the dials; the section's principle is that the payoff is wherever the price has not moved yet.

Those cannot both be the whole story. The asymmetry: CDS at 1,233–1,289 basis points, bonds near 16 percent, against a fork at 0.30 that the loan is pulled, unlaunched, priced above 9 percent or downsized.
